# law of cause and effect

Cause and effect are treated as a basic order of existence: effects depend on causes, and events arise through means and conditions rather than from absolute nothingness. Causes may be linked in chains, with each thing acting on and responding to others. Some texts also distinguish divine causation from the proximate causes observed in nature. The principle applies beyond physical events to moral action, where choices and deeds are said to bear consequences.

Causal chains are illustrated by [interconnectedness; all things involved in all things; chain of being](https://portlandiator.github.io/PI_browser/subjects.html?subject=2FC11A99D6A34DFD93993D59F31450E8), where each being affects and depends on others. The [four causes](https://portlandiator.github.io/PI_browser/subjects.html?subject=B4B44DC18C4C4966909308D87AC31945) offers a more specific framework, distinguishing the efficient, material, formal, and final conditions of an object. [Present and future calamities; war; universal convulsion](https://portlandiator.github.io/PI_browser/subjects.html?subject=8CDDA7D99E83E1421D3C3542A49272FA) links causation to historical events and their effects, while [one universal law; attractive power of love](https://portlandiator.github.io/PI_browser/subjects.html?subject=D190D773F56F4F3CBA7AD064E2098780) describes an ordering force that connects diverse beings. The connection to [superstition](https://portlandiator.github.io/PI_browser/subjects.html?subject=D620FD6DAD8CE0263CC82E624719AE3D) marks a contrast: causal explanation looks for actual means and relationships rather than imagined forces. Together these themes present causality as physical, social, and ethical order.
